The Power of Compound Interest

By Jasper Saunders • Educational content only

Compound interest is the process by which returns generate further returns. Over long periods it becomes the dominant force in building a retirement nest egg. Understanding it is not about memorizing a formula. It is about respecting time and consistency.

How Compounding Actually Works

When your portfolio earns a return, that gain becomes part of the capital that can earn the next return. A 7% gain on $10,000 is $700. The next year, 7% applies to $10,700. The difference seems small at first. Over 20–30 years it is not small.

The three levers are: the amount you contribute, the rate of return, and the number of years. You control contributions and time most directly. Return is less controllable but still influenced by costs, allocation, and behavior.

A Simple Numerical Illustration

Assume $500 per month, 7% average annual return, compounded monthly:

  • 10 years: roughly $86,000 (about $60,000 contributed)
  • 20 years: roughly $260,000 (about $120,000 contributed)
  • 30 years: roughly $610,000 (about $180,000 contributed)

These are illustrative, ignore fees and taxes for clarity, and assume a steady return that real markets do not deliver year by year. The relative lesson holds: the later years dominate because the base has grown.

Why Starting Earlier Matters More Than Most People Expect

Two people contribute the same total dollars. One starts at 25 and stops at 35. The other starts at 35 and continues to 65. Under many reasonable return assumptions, the early starter ends ahead even though they contributed for fewer years. Time in the market compounds the early dollars longer.

If you started late, the response is not despair. It is a higher savings rate, a longer work horizon if needed, and honest stress-testing of the plan. Compounding still works. It just has less runway.

What Breaks Compounding

  • High fees that skim a percentage every year
  • Frequent trading and market timing that interrupts growth
  • Pausing contributions during downturns (often the best time to buy)
  • Withdrawing from long-term capital for short-term wants
  • Lifestyle inflation that permanently raises spending as income rises

Protecting the process is as important as choosing the right expected return.

Use the Growth Projection section of this site to model your contribution rate and years. Then import the projected balance into the retirement stress test. Compounding builds the nest egg; Monte Carlo tests whether it can support the life you want.

Accountability

Compound interest rewards people who fund the future before funding every present impulse. The calculator can show the cost of delay. Only you can close the gap between knowing and doing.

Closing

Respect time. Keep costs low. Contribute consistently. Let compounding do the heavy lifting. That is the simple path most people abandon for complexity that feels more sophisticated and delivers less.
