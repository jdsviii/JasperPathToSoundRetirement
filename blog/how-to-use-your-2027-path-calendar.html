How to Use Your 2027 Path Calendar

By Jasper Saunders • Educational content only

Who this is for - Anyone who wants a calm, once-a-month rhythm for retirement money tasks instead of a December scramble.

What you will leave with - A way to use the 2027 Path Calendar one month at a time, three calculator check-ins (January, June, October), and a plan for what to do if a date slips.

A calendar can be a source of pressure or a source of peace. It depends on how you use it. The 2027 Path Calendar is built to be the second kind: twelve months, five small moves each, and a short list of real deadlines marked in red so they never sneak up on you.

This article is the teacher's note that goes with it. It shows you how to take one month at a time, when to re-run the free Retirement Calculator, how the busy months differ from the quiet ones, and what to do if a date gets away from you. The calendar and the calculators are free. Coaching exists for people who want company on the path, but you never need it to use anything here.

What the calendar is, and what it is not

The calendar is a prompt sheet. Each month has a one-word theme (January is Begin, April is File, December is Finish) and five short moves. A few months also carry hard dates, meaning dates set by an outside agency such as the IRS or Medicare. Those months are marked in red on the sheet.

It is not a tax return, not advice for your situation, and not a promise that your plan will work. Dates can shift with weekends and holidays, and your own facts decide which ones apply to you. The calendar page says "Dates checked: October 2026" for a reason: rules change, so confirm anything that touches your money with IRS.gov, Medicare.gov, SSA.gov, or a qualified professional before you act.

Rule one: one month at a time

Sixty bullets can feel like a mountain if you read them all at once. Do not. Open the sheet on the first of the month, read only that month, and choose. Skipping a bullet that does not apply to you is not failure. It is editing. For example, estimated tax payments only matter if you pay them, and the required-withdrawal items only matter once you reach the age where withdrawals are required.

A simple way to work a month:

  1. Read the five moves for this month and nothing else.
  2. Mark the ones that apply to you. Cross out the ones that do not.
  3. Put any dated item into your phone or calendar app so a reminder finds you.
  4. Do one move this week. Do the rest when you can, in short sessions of 20 or 30 minutes.
  5. Write one line in a plan log: what I did, what I learned, what is next.

That is the whole system. A plan you can finish in small bites beats a perfect plan you never open.

Hard-deadline months and quiet months

Seven months carry at least one date from an outside agency: January, March, April, June, September, October, and December. Those dates do not move because you are busy. The other five (February, May, July, August, and November) have no hard dates on the sheet. Their moves are about gathering, understanding, and preparing.

The idea is simple: use the red months to act and the quiet months to prepare. Quiet months quietly make the red ones lighter. Saving your year-end statements in February makes April easier. Drafting your open enrollment questions in August makes October easier. Asking each custodian how long year-end transfers take in November makes December easier.

Here is what the red months hold, in plain words:

  • January - an estimated tax payment date (Jan 15) and the start of Medicare General Enrollment (Jan 1 to Mar 31), a window for people who missed their first sign-up.
  • March - General Enrollment closes on Mar 31.
  • April - the busiest month: the first required withdrawal date for some people (Apr 1), the federal filing deadline, a quarterly estimate, and the last day for 2026 IRA and HSA contributions (all Apr 15).
  • June and September - the second and third estimated tax payments (Jun 15 and Sep 15).
  • October - the extended filing deadline for people who filed an extension (Oct 15) and the opening of Medicare Open Enrollment (Oct 15), the yearly window to review or change Medicare plans.
  • December - Open Enrollment closes (Dec 7), and year-end deadlines for RMDs and QCDs (Dec 31).

A few terms, defined once. Estimated tax payments are payments you send during the year toward that year's tax when paycheck withholding is not enough; not everyone makes them. An RMD (required minimum distribution) is the yearly withdrawal the IRS requires from certain retirement accounts once you reach a set age, and that age depends on your birth year, so confirm yours at IRS.gov or with your advisor. A QCD (qualified charitable distribution) is a gift sent directly from an IRA to a charity.

Three calculator check-ins: January, June, October

The calendar has a quiet thread running through it: the free Retirement Calculator. It uses Monte Carlo testing, which means running your plan through many different simulated sequences of market returns instead of one smooth average. The success rate is how often the plan lasted across those simulated paths under the rules you entered. It is a teaching number, not a guarantee. (If you want the full picture, read how to interpret your success rate.)

Run it three times a year, in the same way each time:

  1. January - the baseline. Enter honest inputs: your real balances, your real spending (not the number you wish it were), income you can count on, and a return assumption you could defend out loud. Write down the success rate, the median ending balance, and the 10th percentile, which is the result in the tougher markets of your test. Save or screenshot the page.
  2. June - the midyear update. Replace the January balances with today's balances and the spending you actually saw. Leave every input that has not changed alone. Re-run and compare with January.
  3. October - the pre-year-end read. Use fall balances, run it again, and line up all three results. Before December, decide the one change you want to make for next year.

Honest inputs are the whole game. A calculator can only be as honest as the numbers you give it. If you are not sure how to choose them, start with honest inputs and how to choose a mean return.

Change one lever at a time. A lever is one input you control, such as spending, when you retire, or how much you save. If you change three levers at once and the success rate moves, you will not know which one did the work. Change one, re-run, write down what happened, put it back, and only then try the next. This is the same habit the July row of the calendar asks for, and it is the fastest way to learn which lever matters most for you.

If the number drops between January and June, ask "what changed?" before you ask "what is wrong?" Usually the answer is markets, spending, or an input that was a guess in January. If it rises, resist the urge to spend the good news. Write it down and keep going.

The quiet-month tools: guardrails and a cash buffer

Two moves from August deserve a mention because they protect you from yourself. Spending guardrails are preset rules, written while markets are calm, that tell you when to trim spending and when you can raise it. A cash buffer is money set aside in safe cash so that a bad market never forces you to sell investments to pay the bills. Both are easier to build in a quiet month than in the middle of a scare. If you want to go deeper, read about flexible spending and guardrails and cash buffers in early retirement.

What to do if you miss a date

First, breathe. Missing a date is common, and most dates have a next step. The worst response is silence, because problems that are ignored tend to grow. Here is a calm way to handle it:

  1. Get the facts. Which date was it, which agency owns it, and which account or form does it touch?
  2. Go to the source. Read the relevant page at IRS.gov or Medicare.gov, or call your plan's custodian (the company that holds the account). Rules and consequences differ by deadline and by situation, and some have remedies while others do not, so this article will not guess.
  3. Ask a professional for anything that moves real money. A CPA or enrolled agent can tell you what applies to you. For example, the IRS explains correction steps, and in some cases relief, for a missed required withdrawal in its RMD frequently asked questions.
  4. Fix the system, not yourself. Add a reminder, move the date earlier, and write the lesson in your log so it does not repeat.

Shame is not a tool. A missed date is information about your system, and you can adjust the system.

Get the calendar, the PDF, and the reminders

Everything is free, and nothing is sent to anyone. Pick what suits you:

  • The 2027 Path Calendar page - read it on screen, use the print button, and jump straight to a month. For example, June or October.
  • The one-page PDF - print it and put it on the fridge or inside a folder.
  • Deadline reminders (.ics) - a small file you can import into Google Calendar, Apple Calendar, or Outlook. It holds only the hard dates, as all-day events with alerts one week and one day ahead. Weekends and holidays can shift a date, so confirm before you act.

Months at a glance: January, February, March, April, May, June, July, August, September, October, November, December.

From pressure to peace, one month at a time

Retirement planning feels heavy when it arrives as one giant question. The calendar breaks it into small ones that fit inside a month. You do not need to do all of it, and you do not need to do it perfectly. You need a rhythm: read the month, pick the moves that fit, run the calculators when the calendar says to, and write down what you learned.

If you only do one thing this year, pick the month you are in and make one move. That is a path.
